# Nova Scholar: school, parent and educator market study

Date: 17 September 2026. Status: desk research complete; recommendations proposed, not approved implementation. Read alongside the [competitive and Kenya market study](competitive-market-research.md). This report extends and qualifies its original tertiary-first recommendation.

Current direction update, 21 September 2026: the owner has selected school administration plus e-learning and deferred AI priority. This report's market-choice recommendations are historical; use the [active plan](plans/school-platform-implementation.md) for delivery.

Subsequent study (21 September 2026): [school management with connected e-learning](school-management-learning-strategy-study.md) evaluates school administration as the main institutional offer, including day/boarding operations and independent learning subscriptions. Its proposed school-led route is an alternative to this report's direct-parent launch, not an approved scope change.

## 1. The decision in plain language

**Do not target “all students.” Test parent-funded upper-primary learning against the existing adult-tertiary proposition, then choose one initial market.**

My preferred school-market hypothesis is **Grade 5 mathematics support**, purchased by a parent, with educator-reviewed content and short, targeted practice. The wider potential segment is Grades 4–6, but starting with all three grades and every subject would multiply content and quality obligations before demand is established. Grade 5 is a product judgment, not a statistically proven best market: it offers a focused upper-primary learning task without starting in the Grade 6 assessment year.

The strongest promise is:

> Understand where your child needs help, give them appropriate practice, and see what they can now do independently.

Adult tertiary remains the **fastest route using the current implementation**, with fewer child-account and curriculum-authoring prerequisites. Parent-funded learning is a potentially attractive business, not a proven better business. School enrolment and parental concern do not establish willingness to pay, renewal, or affordable acquisition.

Recommended decision rule: choose the family offer only if reachable parents demonstrate repeated use and paid demand, qualified educators can sustain content quality, and child-safety/legal and contribution-margin gates are met. Otherwise continue the adult product pilot. Do not launch two complete products simultaneously.

## 2. What the Kenyan curriculum changes

CBC refers to the competency-based curriculum; CBE describes the broader competency-based education approach, and CBA the assessment approach. A curriculum-aligned product must support application and understanding, not merely relabel multiple-choice revision as competency development.

The education structure distinguishes pre-primary, primary Grades 1–6, junior school Grades 7–9, and senior school Grades 10–12. Use current grade-specific designs when commissioning lessons; the foundational framework alone is not evidence that a subject list or learning outcome remains current. [KICD framework](https://kicd.ac.ke/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/BASIC-EDUCATION-CURRICULUM-FRAMEWORK-2019.pdf), [current curriculum catalogue](https://kicd.ac.ke/cbc-materials/curriculum-designs/regular-curriculum-designs/).

The transition matters commercially: the first senior-school cohort entered Grade 10 in 2026. Senior pathways include STEM, Social Sciences, and Arts and Sports. Do not model all three senior grades as already populated by this cohort in 2026, or treat legacy Form/KCSE resources as interchangeable with Grade 10 content. [Ministry placement statement, December 2025](https://education.go.ke/sites/default/files/2025-12/PS%20BITOK%20-%20STATUS%20OF%20THE%20ONGOING%20PLACEMENT%20OF%20GRADE%20NINE%20LEARNERS%20%281%29.pdf).

KNEC identifies KPSEA at Grade 6 and KJSEA at Grade 9, and describes the planned end of KCSE in 2027. This makes legacy KCSE revision a transitional opportunity rather than a durable positioning choice. The KNEC history page was available through its indexed official text, but full-page retrieval failed during this review. [KNEC history](https://www.knec.ac.ke/who-we-are/history/).

Proposed curriculum requirements, subject to educator verification:

- Tag each resource by curriculum version, grade, learning area, strand/sub-strand and specific outcome; record reviewer and review date.
- Combine worked examples, practice, explanations and appropriate practical activities. Distinguish a correct answer from demonstrated understanding.
- Use reviewed rubrics for open responses; escalate uncertain grading instead of inventing precision.
- Show practice progress, not official assessment grades or predicted placement guarantees. Nova Scholar is not an authorized school assessment submission system.
- Check publisher permissions. Public access to a curriculum or textbook does not automatically authorize copying commercial learning content.

KICD publishes a Grade 5 design collection including mathematics. A qualified educator should select and map the initial topics against the applicable design before content production; this study has not audited every mathematics outcome. [Grade 5 designs](https://kicd.ac.ke/cbc-materials/curriculum-designs/grade-five-designs/).

## 3. Which learners should we target?

The following is strategic analysis, not measured market preference.

| Segment | Main buyer and need | Product burden or risk | Recommendation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-primary and Grades 1–3 | Parent; foundational literacy/numeracy and guided activities | Adult co-use, early-reading UX, audio/visual content, developmental suitability | Defer; not a simple adaptation of text chat |
| Grades 4–6 | Parent; specific learning gaps and structured home practice | Curriculum review, shared devices, guardian controls, understandable progress | Best school hypothesis; test Grade 5 maths first |
| Grades 7–9 | Parent, sometimes school; subject understanding and junior-school assessment preparation | More subject specialists, exam competition, device access and learner motivation | Strong alternative if interviews show better access and demand |
| Grades 10–12 | Parent/school; pathway-specific understanding and practical learning | Evolving cohort coverage, specialist content and practical assessment limits | Later, by selected pathway and subject |
| Legacy Forms/KCSE | Parent/learner; immediate exam revision | Time-limited curriculum runway and established competitors | Optional narrow campaign, not core identity |
| Adult university/college | Learner; course-note understanding and effective revision | Fragmented course content, free AI alternatives, student affordability | Best reuse of existing product; retain as comparison arm |
| Adult TVET | Learner/institution; theory linked to occupational competence | Practical competence cannot be established by quizzes alone | Separate discovery, not automatic university copy |

“School” also hides important differences. Day versus boarding arrangements, public versus private schools, shared versus personal phones, parental time, language preferences, disability/access needs and rural versus urban connectivity can change adoption. Recruit across these circumstances; do not equate school category with ability to pay.

### Market size without an inflated revenue claim

KNBS's indexed 2026 Facts and Figures school-enrolment table reports 2025 primary enrolment of 4,192.2 thousand male and 4,042.4 thousand female learners, and junior enrolment of 1,898.1 thousand male and 1,803.0 thousand female learners. These sum to **8,234,600 primary** and **3,701,100 junior** learners: **11,935,700 combined**. Full PDF retrieval failed, so these are explicitly indexed official figures, not a complete table audit. They are not Grade 5 counts, households, connected users or paying customers. [KNBS Facts and Figures 2026](https://www.knbs.or.ke/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/2026-Facts-and-Figures.pdf).

Do not multiply these totals by a subscription price. Estimate the reachable market from specific recruitment partners, eligible households, device access, activated learners and actual paid conversion. Siblings can share one payer; one household may also have multiple phones. Adult internet statistics in the original study cannot establish children's access.

## 5. Parents: holiday learning and help with difficult subjects

There are two different jobs to test:

1. **Ongoing catch-up:** “My child struggles with this topic; what can we do this week?” Potentially recurring, but parent time and learner resistance may limit use.
2. **School-break continuity:** “I want worthwhile, manageable activities while school is closed.” Potentially easier scheduling, but demand may disappear when term resumes.

Do not assume more screen time is the desired outcome. Offer short sessions with an optional practical or printable activity, manageable goals and no public ranking. Download/offline features would require a separate approved scope change because the current BRD excludes offline learning.

Proposed experience: parent chooses grade and goal → learner completes a small reviewed diagnostic → product proposes one achievable topic → explanation and guided attempt → independent practice → delayed reassessment → parent receives a weekly summary of demonstrated progress and one helpful activity. Avoid labelling a child “weak”; say “needs more practice with …”.

The parent view should answer what was attempted, what was demonstrated independently, what remains uncertain and how to help. Do not equate minutes, streaks or repeated identical questions with mastery. Report limitations rather than guaranteed grade improvement.

APHRC's account of a community parenting intervention supports treating parental involvement as important context. Its qualitative endline involved 126 respondents in a specific Nairobi informal-settlement programme, not a national app-demand survey. Reported improvements in engagement do **not** establish that parents will purchase Nova Scholar or that an app causes the same outcomes. [APHRC parental involvement study](https://aphrc.org/publication/the-power-of-community-interventions-in-promoting-parental-involvement/).

### Holiday tuition is a legal gate, not just a marketing choice

Section 37 of the Basic Education Act contains the wording “No pupil shall be subjected to holiday tuition.” This was checked against indexed Kenya Law text; direct consolidated-page retrieval returned an access error. This research has not determined how the provision applies to the exact proposed self-paced or educator-led online service. **Obtain current Kenyan legal advice before launching a paid holiday teaching offer.** Calling it “home learning” does not establish an exemption, and the provision should not be interpreted here as a blanket ban on all home practice. [Basic Education Act, indexed 2022 consolidation](https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/2013/14/eng%402022-12-31/source).

## 6. Educators: tools first, marketplace later

| Model | Value | Operational burden | Sequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Commissioned, reviewed lesson library | Reliable content parents can use immediately | Pay authors/reviewers, secure rights, maintain curriculum versions | First school prototype |
| Educator workspace | Draft worksheets, rubrics, explanations and assignments; review before publishing | Teacher usability, permissions, feedback and revision workflow | Narrow pilot with invited reviewers |
| Paid feedback or small cohorts | Human help for cases automation cannot resolve | Availability, safeguarding, service levels, pricing and refunds | Later controlled experiment, subject to legal review |
| Open resource marketplace | Educators sell original tools and lesson packs | Quality moderation, rights disputes, discovery, payouts and tax treatment | Defer until demand and supply are demonstrated |
| Open live-tutor marketplace | Parent books a suitable tutor | Vetting, scheduling, attendance, child contact, disputes and settlements | Last; not an extension of chat alone |

Start with a few commissioned educators, not a promise that a two-sided marketplace will fill itself. Suggested workflow: select outcome → draft lesson/items → independent educator review → publish version → assign → inspect anonymized item problems → revise with an audit trail. AI may draft; it should not approve its own curriculum accuracy or answer keys.

Test teacher tools separately from parent subscriptions. An educator may use free authoring tools while parents fund reviewed learning; schools may instead pay for assignment/reporting workflows. These are different buyers with different acquisition cycles. Avoid bundling school administration into the consumer pilot.

## 7. Child safety and account boundaries

ODPC's children's-data guidance emphasizes children's best interests, lawful processing, appropriate parental/guardian involvement, age/authority verification, transparency and safeguards. Consent alone is not a universal permission to collect everything. Assess registration obligations, high-risk processing/DPIA needs, third-party transfers and provider handling before enrolling children. A legal/privacy review must establish the requirements applicable to this service. [ODPC guidance, 2025](https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/ODPC-%E2%80%93-Guidance-Note-for-Processing-Childrens-Data.pdf).

Proposed product safeguards—not a claim that these are all mandated verbatim:

- Separate payer/guardian, learner and educator roles. Verify authority proportionately and minimize identity data; do not default to collecting national-ID images.
- Use child profiles without requiring personal child email; protect profile switching on shared devices and prevent one sibling viewing another's work.
- Give educators access only to assigned learning work. Parent progress visibility should be defined, not assumed to mean unrestricted surveillance of every conversation.
- Restrict open-ended AI and adult–child messaging; provide safe escalation and incident reporting. No public profiles, behavioural advertising or open direct messages in the pilot.
- Explain deletion, retention, correction and provider processing in age-appropriate language. Use synthetic examples until lawful research participation is ready.
- Obtain appropriate guardian permission and child assent for research. A school introduction or adult account is not sufficient evidence of authority over every learner.

These capabilities are **not implemented by this research**. Existing adult authorization and subscriptions are useful foundations, not proof that the application is ready for minors.

## 8. Commercial model and affordability

The approved project baseline remains Student KES 299 and Pro KES 699; it contains no approved family plan. Proposed experiments:

- Test KES 299 versus KES 499 monthly for the **same clearly bounded one-learner, one-subject offer** across exploratory parent cohorts. These are hypotheses, not competitor-derived market prices or a statistically powered price test.
- Compare a four-week topic pack with a subscription to learn whether the problem is episodic. Clearly explain access expiry and renewal; do not disguise recurring charges.
- Consider sibling bundles only after measuring incremental content, AI and support costs. Do not include unlimited individual educator attention in a low-price automated plan.
- Keep paid human feedback separately priced with explicit limits. No payout percentage is recommended without local tax, payment and settlement review.

Illustration only: 100 households paying KES 299 generate KES 29,900 gross in a month, not profit. Subtract tax obligations where applicable, payment/refund costs, AI/hosting, educator creation and review, support and acquisition. Track per-household and per-active-child costs separately. Commissioned content has upfront cost and continuing maintenance; “reusable” does not mean free.

Prefer a measured partner/referral pilot to broad paid advertising. A trusted educator may introduce parents, but never pressure children to buy or disclose class contact lists without permission. Compare parent-led recruitment with school introductions; formal school procurement may be slower and must not be mistaken for direct-parent demand.

## 9. A fair validation study before choosing the build

No interviews, prototype trials or payments were conducted in this desk study. Suggested stages:

**Discovery:** interview 12 adult tertiary learners, 12 parents and 6 educators. Include upper-primary and junior-school educators, varied household access, and both holiday and term-time needs. Ask about the most recent difficulty, existing spending/substitutes, device access and actions taken—not just whether an imagined product sounds useful. This small sample is exploratory, not representative.

**Comparable prototypes:** demonstrate one adult course-to-practice workflow and one reviewed Grade 5 maths workflow. Use synthetic learner profiles for adult-led evaluation first. Assess whether parents understand the report and whether teachers agree the items actually measure the intended outcome. Do not recruit children into the current app before the safeguards are ready.

**Four-week pilot, if approved and ready:** aim for 20 adult learners and 20 eligible families, each with one clearly defined learning goal. Use similar acquisition effort and support intensity; record differences. Extend family observation into term time if the pilot occurs during a holiday. Collect real paid signals only after payment, refund and legal gates; otherwise label willingness-to-pay responses as stated preference.

Provisional management gates, to agree before launch:

| Question | Evidence to collect | Suggested decision gate—not an industry benchmark |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can people start? | Independent completion of the first learning cycle | At least 14 of 20 in each arm, reporting assisted starts separately |
| Do they return? | Meaningful practice in week 4 | At least 10 of 20 enrolled participants, not just completers |
| Will buyers pay? | Actual purchase/renewal at the disclosed offer | At least 6 of 20 eligible buyers; no claiming success from survey intent |
| Is learning credible? | Reviewed unseen pre/post items and delayed checks | Report individual change and errors; no causal efficacy claim from this small uncontrolled pilot |
| Can we deliver safely? | Content review, access controls, incident handling | No unresolved critical safety or answer-key issues; all required reviews complete |
| Can economics work? | Contribution after variable delivery/support costs; acquisition payback estimate | Positive measured contribution and an owner-approved feasible acquisition plan |

Do not compare raw maths scores with university course scores. Compare each participant against their own starting point, and compare business viability using revenue, cost, retention and recruitment effort. Passing these gates justifies a larger experiment, not nationwide market fit. If both offers fail, revise the problem/offer before adding more features.

## 10. How this changes the implementation decision

**Next step is segment validation and owner scope review—not an automatic move to P6 payments or a school marketplace.**

If adult tertiary wins: continue P3 ingestion quality, P4 grounded answers/citations and P5 reviewed practice, then meet P2/P6 cost and payment gates. The original report's adult pilot becomes the next larger trial.

If the family offer wins: first approve revised requirements and a separate school-product plan covering guardian/child permissions, curriculum/content ownership, educator review, privacy/safeguarding, economics and revised acceptance tests. Then deliver one safe learning cycle. Reuse identity, queues, billing foundations and practice concepts where appropriate, but do not assume arbitrary uploaded course notes are the best starting content for children.

Defer all-grade coverage, school ERP, open marketplaces, video conferencing and full offline support. Any acceptance of these proposals must update requirements, decisions, architecture/data model and phase plans before implementation. This report intentionally leaves the currently accepted adult scope unchanged.

## 11. Evidence limits and owner decisions

Research combines official Kenyan curriculum/regulatory/statistical sources, vendor descriptions and explicitly labelled product judgments. Undated product pages are a research-date snapshot. No market-share, willingness-to-pay, product accuracy, legal clearance or learning-outcome claim has been independently established. The KNBS, KNEC history and Kenya Law retrieval limitations are disclosed where used; verify full current documents before investment or launch decisions that depend on them.

Owner decisions after reading:

1. Approve comparison of the adult offer with Grade 5 maths, or choose a different narrow grade/subject based on accessible participants.
2. Identify a recruitment owner and qualified educator reviewers; set a discovery and content-review budget.
3. Decide whether to commission the child-service legal/privacy review before any learner pilot.
4. Choose one implementation focus after evidence is collected; approve a revised plan before changing scope.

**Bottom line:** parent-funded learning deserves a serious test, especially specific-subject catch-up. Nova Scholar should compete on trusted learning progress and useful parent guidance—not breadth, AI branding, or a promise to cover every Kenyan learner immediately.
